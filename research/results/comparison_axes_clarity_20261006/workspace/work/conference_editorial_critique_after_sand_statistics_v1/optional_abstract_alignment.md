## Current local title (verbatim)

Adaptive Interaction Graphs for Particle Simulation

## Current local abstract (verbatim)

Particle graph-network simulators reconstruct local interaction graphs from a fixed geometric rule, even when prediction difficulty varies across a scene. We study a lightweight controller that uses the preceding step's predicted residual scale to allocate extra local edges. A reproducibility audit shows why three quantities must be distinguished: predicted residual risk, the benefit of additional interactions, and the geometry-dependent cost of a free rollout. In particular, an expansion policy cannot reduce edges on identical positions, and accurate residual prediction does not guarantee useful allocation. We formulate an exact incremental pair budget, apply established faithful heteroscedastic regression to preserve the mean predictor's squared-error gradients, and derive a conditional allocation-regret bound separating estimation error, temporal lag, and risk--benefit mismatch. Reanalysis of released rollouts does not resolve the previously reported WaterDrop accuracy advantage at step 200 and reverses its sign over the full saved horizon. New controlled CPU experiments evaluate alternative objectives and matched-budget policies on a small public WaterDrop subset. The resulting evidence supports a reproducible study of adaptive computation, while leaving full-scale accuracy--runtime superiority unestablished.

## Optional aligned abstract

Particle graph-network simulators build local interaction graphs geometrically. We study whether a simulator's residual predictions can guide useful additional interactions. A paired graph-exposure intervention trains base-only and mixed-graph simulators before comparing allocation policies at an explicit optional-pair budget. We adopt faithful heteroscedastic regression to control mean fitting and derive a conditional allocation bound separating score error, temporal lag and risk-benefit mismatch. Fresh 100k-update Goop and Sand studies and paired WaterDrop continuations from 100k to 110k distinguish fixed-policy training effects, observed-history placement and autonomous feedback. Graph exposure improves fixed-random rollout error in every Goop and WaterDrop seed, but only two of three Sand seeds. Risk remains worse than random on Goop observed test histories; WaterDrop reverses this ordering on observed test histories but has mixed autonomous interactions. Sand narrows the observed risk deficit yet worsens cached-risk rollout error and its risk-minus-random gap in every seed. We retain failed outcomes, undefined full-horizon comparisons, physical mismatches and measured costs. These exploratory results show that graph exposure can improve a fixed expansion policy, while residual ranking alone does not establish useful allocation or accuracy-runtime superiority.

## Why a draft is warranted

The current abstract describes a small CPU WaterDrop pilot as the new experimental evidence, while the main paper now centers paired full-architecture Goop/Sand training and WaterDrop continuation. This is material scope drift, not evidence that its adverse historical statement or no-superiority conclusion is false. The candidate foregrounds the constructive training intervention and complete conditional/adverse allocation findings. Historical reanalysis and the compact pilot remain in the paper; their omission from this proposed abstract changes emphasis, not evidence. “Lightweight” is removed because the cached-score design does not establish end-to-end efficiency.

The candidate does not claim a clean cross-material intervention, pristine confirmation, convergence, state-of-the-art performance or stable physical simulation.
