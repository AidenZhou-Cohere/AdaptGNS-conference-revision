# Narrative recommendation

Make the paper answer one question: **Can a cheap prediction of local error tell a particle simulator where additional interactions will help?** Recover the original's physical opening: splash and impact regions plausibly need more interaction information, while quiet regions may not. Present cached residual scale as an appealing candidate, then distinguish identifying difficulty from selecting an effective intervention. The contribution is evidence about that distinction, not an inventory of corrections.

1. **Why investigate this controller?** Start the experiments with the original Sand/WaterDrop comparisons. Preserve the favorable comparisons against the fixed legacy NLL model and the weaker comparisons against MSE baselines. The horizon reversal and geometry-dependent edge counts create a scientific puzzle: apparent gains do not yet establish that the score places edges usefully. Keep the historical table visible, with one concise provenance limitation.

2. **Does predicting error imply predicting the value of extra messages?** Introduce the paired full-model objective controls before presenting their common-state results. NLL can change mean fitting, so faithful training tests whether objective coupling explains the allocation result. Holding history and optional-pair count fixed distinguishes placement from extra computation and changed rollout geometry. Present residual versus benefit correlations together with random/speed comparisons: correlations alone cannot establish a useful selector.

3. **Are we asking the wrong benefit question?** Dense expansion and a selected quarter of optional pairs are different interventions. Motivate the actual-action analysis with that mismatch, then show that replacing the dense proxy does not rescue risk ranking. Its positive high-risk-quartile benefit deserves space alongside random's larger benefit there. This avoids turning a nuanced result into “risk contains no information.”

4. **Could graph conventions or training exposure explain the gap?** First restore trained self-messages and preserve the native base graph: absolute accuracy changes, so this is a substantive control. Then use the random-draw envelope to test dependence on one random comparator, and paired continuation to test expanded-graph exposure—only after those results are complete and audited. These controls delimit the conclusion; they cannot retroactively repair the original evaluation.

5. **What changes when risk chooses the graph?** Use the prediction-change decomposition to explain the remaining pattern: improved residual alignment can coexist with a larger perturbation penalty. State that policy-defined exposure groups describe the gap; they do not identify a causal mechanism. If space is tight, retain the identity and qualitative observation in the main text and move detailed groups to the appendix.

6. **Does local allocation yield a useful simulator?** End with autonomous full-rollout error, failures, boundaries and measured runtime. This answers the opening practical question after the reader understands what the controller can and cannot exploit. Report paired seeds as the replication unit, not hundreds of outcomes.

Move checksums, recovery histories, exact completion counts, file formats, correction chronology and detailed selection provenance to appendices. Retain brief main-text disclosures of inspected-data reuse, graph shift and exploratory status. Keep title, abstract and every numerical table unchanged; organize theory around the experimental questions.
