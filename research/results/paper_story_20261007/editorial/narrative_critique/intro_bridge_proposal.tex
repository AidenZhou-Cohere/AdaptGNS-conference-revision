Before asking where to add interactions, the simulator must learn how to use them. We therefore pair base-only training with exposure to random graph expansion, then freeze each model and compare allocation policies. The experiments follow three questions: does exposure improve the use of a fixed expansion policy, does residual risk choose better edges on a common state, and do those choices remain useful when the simulator advances its own predictions? This ordering separates the training intervention from the allocation decision and its subsequent feedback.

Figure~\ref{fig:goop-exposure-placement} captures the resulting distinction. Graph exposure improves Goop under random expansion, yet residual ranking still allocates the same budget worse than random on matched histories. WaterDrop shows that this placement ordering can reverse; Sand shows that an improved observed comparison need not survive autonomous prediction. The method and experiments thus focus on what an adaptive controller must establish: the added messages are useful to its model, the score predicts their benefit, and the resulting trajectory remains reliable.
