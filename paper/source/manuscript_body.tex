\fancyhead[CE,CO]{\small\bfseries Adaptive Interaction Graphs for Particle Simulation}

\begin{abstract}
Larger interaction graphs give a particle simulator more context, but every added edge requires more message passing. We ask whether selective expansion can preserve accuracy while adding fewer edges. AdaptGNS retains the native graph and selects at most a quarter of the nearby candidate pairs, using uncertainty from the previous prediction to guide the choice. Training with randomly expanded graphs prepares the simulator for these additions. In three-seed studies of Goop, WaterDrop and Sand, we test whether a small expansion budget preserves accuracy, and whether uncertainty spends that budget better than random placement. For mixed-trained WaterDrop models, quarter-budget graphs on shared test histories use about 25\% fewer total messages than dense expansion and achieve lower next-step error in every seed. Both random and uncertainty-guided placement achieve this result. Training with expansion also reduces Goop's full-rollout MSE from 0.301 to 0.129 under the same random policy. Uncertainty guidance offers no consistent advantage across settings; on Sand, expansion training worsens uncertainty-guided rollouts. The evidence supports selective expansion as a way to improve the accuracy--edge tradeoff, while showing that predicting difficulty alone does not identify the best additional interactions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A splash may need more interaction partners than a quietly moving patch of fluid. Enlarging every particle's neighborhood gives both regions more messages, whether or not those messages improve the forecast. For a learned particle simulator\citep{sanchez2020}, the aim is therefore not simply to maximize connectivity: it is to preserve useful accuracy while conserving the extra interactions. Can we obtain the benefits of a larger neighborhood without paying for every candidate edge?

AdaptGNS explores this question through selective graph expansion. It keeps the simulator's native connections and spends a limited allowance of extra pairs. Predicted uncertainty provides an intuitive allocation rule: give more context to particles the model finds difficult. A residual head supplies that signal, and caching its preceding output avoids a separate scoring pass at each forecast. The uncertainty maps are visually compelling because difficulty is spatially structured. The question is whether using that structure makes a limited edge budget more useful.

Our experiments distinguish two sources of value: \textbf{using fewer additions than dense expansion, and choosing those additions better than a simple budget-matched rule.} We find a clear example of the first in WaterDrop. With mixed training on common observed histories, a quarter-annulus budget lowers next-step error relative to dense expansion while using about 25\% fewer total messages. Both uncertainty-guided and random placement achieve this result in every seed. The savings belong to selective expansion; attributing an additional gain to uncertainty requires the random control.

Training also changes what that allowance buys. Under the same random-expansion policy, graph exposure improves autonomous forecasts in every paired Goop and WaterDrop seed. Yet uncertainty does not consistently improve the placement of those messages, and Sand shows that narrowing the observed risk deficit can accompany worse rollouts once predictions feed back into future graphs. We organize the paper around this path from an edge budget to a useful forecast (Figure~\ref{fig:constructive-overview}): compare sparse and dense expansion, examine the uncertainty signal, and test training, placement and feedback. The goal remains economical interaction graphs; the evidence shows which parts of that design currently deliver.

\section{Related work}
Graph networks learn interactions\citep{battaglia2016}; GNS combines geometric particle graphs with message passing and noise-augmented training\citep{sanchez2020}. We build on released particle GNS software\citep{kumar2023}. Neural SPH incorporates physical structure into fluid prediction\citep{toshev2024}. Our intervention changes communication while retaining each trained simulator for a policy comparison.

MeshGraphNets predicts a sizing field for remeshing\citep{pfaff2021}, while LAMP learns refinement and coarsening under an error--computation tradeoff\citep{wu2023}. We retain the particle set and add local pairs. NRI and dNRI infer relational structure\citep{kipf2018,graber2020}; curvature-based rewiring addresses communication bottlenecks\citep{topping2022}; AdaMeshNet changes connectivity within message-passing layers\citep{seo2025}. These approaches motivate adaptation without equating prediction difficulty with the value of an additional edge. Faithful regression\citep{stirn2023} addresses the mean--variance optimization problem studied by \citet{seitzer2022}; we adopt its mean-gradient separation as a control.

\section{Budgeted graph adaptation}
\subsection{An allowance for additional interactions}
Our AdaptGNS formulation receives six consecutive frames $H_t$, particle attributes and boundary information. It predicts normalized acceleration and a positive residual score for each particle; acceleration is transformed back to physical coordinates before integration. Let $E_0(x_t)$ be its native directed graph, including self-messages and the receiver cap of 128. We preserve this graph and collect optional unordered pairs in a surrounding annulus. If $P_\rho(x_t)$ contains the unordered geometric pairs inside radius $\rho$, then
\[
 C_R(x_t)=P_R(x_t)\setminus P_r(x_t),\qquad R>r.
\]
The geometric boundary convention is specified in Appendix~\ref{sec:implementation-details}.
For an integer budget $B_t\ge0$, choose $S_t\subseteq C_R(x_t)$ with $|S_t|=\min(B_t,|C_R(x_t)|)$. Each selected pair adds both orientations, $D(S_t)=\{(i,j),(j,i):\{i,j\}\in S_t\}$, so
\begin{equation}
 E_t=E_0(x_t)\cup D(S_t),\qquad |E_t|=|E_0(x_t)|+2|S_t|.
 \label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
Our budgeted policies use $B_t=\lfloor0.25|C_R(x_t)|\rfloor$. They therefore add exactly the same number of messages when evaluated on common positions. Short-range pairs omitted by the native cap are not restored by the annulus, and additions are not recapped; final receiver degrees may exceed 128. Relative to dense expansion on the same positions, this omits at least 75\% of optional messages while keeping every native message. It does not remove 75\% of the whole graph. We evaluate this single quarter budget alongside the native and dense endpoints; graph construction and selection also contribute to runtime.

The policy determines placement within this common action space. Random25 samples uniformly; Speed25 scores displacement magnitude; Risk25 uses predicted residual scores. RMS25 uses relative velocity over native neighbors. Native base and dense expansion provide the no-addition and all-annulus controls. All six policies are used for Goop and Sand; RMS25 was not evaluated in the WaterDrop study. The radius ratio is 1.267. Appendix~\ref{sec:implementation-details} specifies pair ranking, tie handling and graph conventions.

\subsection{Learning to use additional messages}
Base-only training always uses the native graph. In the paired mixed-graph arm, each training example independently receives a uniformly sampled quarter-annulus expansion with probability $1/2$. The two arms share initialization or continuation parent, sampled frames, noise, architecture and optimization schedule. Thus changing the training graph is the intervention; the objective does not reward an allocation policy directly.

We use established faithful regression\citep{stirn2023} to learn residual scores without reweighting the mean predictor's squared-error gradient on matched inputs and graphs. The score $q_i$ estimates per-coordinate squared residual in normalized acceleration units, so $dq_i$ corresponds to vector squared error. It is neither an isolated epistemic-uncertainty estimate nor an estimate of the benefit of expansion. The adopted loss, stop-gradient construction, gradient handling and normalization are given in Appendix~\ref{sec:implementation-details}.

\subsection{Allocating with a cache}
The autonomous residual controller ranks each optional pair by its larger cached endpoint score. One native-base pass on the initial six observed frames initializes the cache. Each forecast then selects pairs, runs the simulator once, advances positions and caches the new scores. The budget applies from forecast 1; later forecasts require no additional scoring pass and use no future positions or labels.

The cache reuses information the simulator has already produced rather than requesting a new prediction solely for allocation. Current positions determine which pairs are eligible, while preceding scores determine their rank. This creates a temporal lag: the controller can respond to the latest available residual signal, but that signal may be stale when motion changes rapidly.

The source of the score matters. During autonomous motion it comes from the controller's own preceding selected graph. For an observed-history comparison, each policy receives the same current history and risk is instead scored on the preceding observed base history. This second setting removes differences in predicted positions and permits a direct comparison of placement. It does not reproduce the feedback encountered by the autonomous controller.

\section{What makes an added interaction worth its cost?}
An edge budget turns allocation into a question of value: how much does a chosen addition improve the forecast? Residual risk estimates error under the current model and graph. For a fixed model, history $H_t$ and target $Y$, the realized benefit of adding pairs $S$ is instead
\begin{align}
 \Delta(S;H_t,Y)={}&\mathcal E(f(H_t,E_0(x_t)),Y)\nonumber\\
 &-\mathcal E(f(H_t,E_0(x_t)\cup D(S)),Y).
 \label{eq:intervention-benefit}
\end{align}
Both predictions use the same loss and target; positive benefit means expansion helped. For vector squared error, write the base residual as $r=Y-f(H_t,E_0)$ and the prediction change as $\delta=f(H_t,E_0\cup D(S))-f(H_t,E_0)$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \Delta=2r^\top\delta-\|\delta\|^2.
 \label{eq:benefit-alignment}
\end{equation}
The alignment gain must exceed the squared magnitude of the prediction change. Predicting the size of the residual supplies neither the direction nor the size of the response to a new neighbor. Large error may reflect missing interaction information, but it may also reflect ambiguity or model bias that extra messages cannot correct.

Benefit belongs to the selected set, not necessarily to each edge independently. Message passing can combine or oppose information from several additions. A per-particle error score lacks both a direction for changing a prediction and an explicit measure of these interactions. The budget fixes the action size; it is the response to that action that the experiments assess.

The WaterDrop diagnostic in Figure~\ref{fig:uncertainty-associations} shows this mismatch under both objectives. Relative to random placement, risk allocation's greater alignment with the target is outweighed by its larger squared prediction change in every seed. Restoring trained self-messages lowers error without reversing the risk--random ordering (Appendix~\ref{sec:actual-action-benefit}). Conditional score-perturbation bounds require the residual score to approximate useful action values; that assumption is not supplied by residual calibration (Appendix~\ref{sec:allocation-score-conditions}).

Computation also requires a common-state comparison. Adding pairs increases the number of directed messages on that state. A smaller graph later in a rollout can result from a different predicted geometry rather than cheaper placement. Caching removes a separate subsequent scoring pass, while construction, selection and extra messages still cost work. Different geometries and shared-host execution prevent an isolated-speedup conclusion; Appendix~\ref{sec:physical-cost} gives the decomposition and measured costs.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
The evidence comes from three-seed exploratory comparisons and includes continuations from inspected parents. Different data, horizons and backends do not isolate material effects; Sand's native-CUDA lineage does not establish optimization equivalence. Radius thresholds and top-budget selection can be discontinuous, and extra edges can amplify error. Neither the conditional theory nor lower MSE establishes stable physical simulation. Conservation, peak memory and optimized runtime remain unmeasured.

The purpose of adaptive interaction graphs is to preserve useful accuracy with a limited allowance of additional messages. WaterDrop demonstrates that a quarter-annulus graph can outperform dense expansion on common observed histories, while graph exposure improves fixed-random autonomous forecasts in Goop and WaterDrop. These results give selective expansion a concrete role. They also locate the remaining difficulty: residual uncertainty does not reliably identify which additions are worth making, and narrowing an observed placement deficit need not improve autonomous forecasts. Training for the graph changes, retaining simple equal-budget controls, and measuring the resulting trajectories are essential to judging the tradeoff. Learning interaction benefit directly is a promising next step.
